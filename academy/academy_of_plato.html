The Academy of Plato

What this activity covers

Athens, 399 BC, during the month of the sacred ship. The verdict is in, the sentence waits on the ship's return from Delos, and the city argues with itself. You walk out through the northwest gate to the grove of Akademos, where the young note-taker from the trial spends his afternoons. He will not lecture you. He will smooth a patch of sand, draw a square, and re-run for you a lesson he once watched Socrates give, in which someone who never studied geometry finds a real geometric truth by answering questions. You play the answerer. Walk with the arrow keys, press E to talk and to take your place by the sand, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Discussion questions

1. Children acquire the grammar of their first language without anyone stating its rules, and infants show expectations about number and objects before any teaching. Do modern cases like these support anything like Plato's claim that some knowledge is in us from the start? Where does the comparison hold, and where does it break?

2. Meno's paradox says you cannot search for what you do not know, since you would not recognize it if you found it. Researchers search for unknown things constantly. Describe how a scientist, a detective, or anyone else searches for something they cannot yet describe, and what stands in for recognition when they find it.

3. A standing objection to the lesson is that the questions quietly taught the answer. Take one of the questions asked at the sand and rewrite it so that it teaches less and tests more. What did you have to remove?
