Pascal's Wager

What this activity covers

The abbey's gatehouse, and the region's road jumps again: it is now around 1660, and the traveler sheltering at the gatehouse table tonight is Blaise Pascal, mathematician, inventor, and religious thinker, with dice and a stack of coins in front of him. He helped invent the mathematics of probability to settle gambling disputes, and in his notebooks he applied it to the largest question there is. He will teach you how to price a bet, then lay out the famous wager on the table, cell by cell, and you will attack it where you think it is weakest. Walk with the arrow keys, press E to talk and to take your seat at the table, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Discussion questions

1. W. K. Clifford wrote that it is wrong, always and everywhere, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, and William James answered that when evidence is silent and the option is live, forced, and momentous, we may choose by our passional nature. State both positions fairly, then take a side and defend it.

2. Infinite payoffs do strange things to decision theory: any nonzero probability times infinity wins, no matter how small the probability. Construct a case where this leads to an absurd conclusion, and say what that shows about the wager's mathematics.

3. Wager-style arguments appear today wherever stakes are huge and evidence is disputed, in debates about catastrophic risks, insurance, and precaution. Pick one modern example and evaluate it: does it inherit the wager's strengths, its weaknesses, or both?
