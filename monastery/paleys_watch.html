The Watch on the Heath

What this activity covers

The road beyond the gatehouse climbs onto open heath, and the region's road takes its last jump. It is now around 1800, and your walking companion is William Paley, archdeacon and author, working out the book he will publish in 1802 as Natural Theology. His argument begins with two objects on this ground, a stone and a watch, and with the different answers each one will bear. When you find the watch you will open the case and look at the works while they run. Walk with the arrow keys, press E at each marked spot, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Discussion questions

1. The most discussed modern descendant of the design argument concerns the constants of physics, which appear finely set for the possibility of life. State the fine tuning argument as strongly as you can, then state the strongest reply to it.

2. Hume observed that the universe resembles an animal or a vegetable at least as much as it resembles a machine, and that generation and growth differ from manufacture. How much of the design argument survives if the analogy is changed from watchmaking to gardening?

3. The objection that a designer would itself need a designer resembles the objection to first cause arguments that everything needs a cause. Are the two objections equally strong, and do the two arguments have equally good replies?
