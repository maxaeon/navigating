# Travel Stops

Situations on the roads between regions. They are not destinations and they are not chosen: the world map places them on the routes, and the traveler meets them on the way. This folder holds the first one.

# The Bridge Crossing

A single-file scene activity on the road north, and the deliberate harder twin of the prologue's trolley. A footbridge crosses a gorge, a runaway trolley is coming down the line below toward five workers in a cutting, there is no lever and no side track, and the only thing on the bridge heavy enough to stop the trolley is the large man at the railing. Everything is contained in `footbridge_crossing.html`. There is no walking: the scene unfolds card by card over one drawn view, with the trolley rolling in when the situation calls it.

Stage 0 has the student record their lever answer from the prologue before anything happens, so the pair of answers exists on the record. The situation is then stated, with the background box crediting Philippa Foot's original 1967 case and Judith Jarvis Thomson's 1976 footbridge, and Stage 1 is the decision itself, with refusing both cases among the options. Stage 2 puts the famous asymmetry to the student: most people pull the lever and refuse to push at identical arithmetic, and the options name the leading diagnoses, contact, means versus side effect, and inconsistency, plus rejecting the lever answer instead. The doctrine of double effect is then given with the cleanest version of the test, whether the plan still works if the one person survives, and Stage 3 asks whether intending differs morally from foreseeing. Stage 4 is the required written answer: set the two recorded answers side by side, and either state the principle that covers both, name the one that changes, or defend keeping both without a principle.

The report prints the pair of answers together. The discussion questions ask the student to design a third variant isolating a single difference, to consider who should encode such answers into vehicles and protocols before emergencies, and to weigh whether principle-less pairs of judgments indict the judge or the demand for principles. On completion the activity writes the key `phil_map_footbridge`, and the world map shows the crossing made.

## Accessibility

Objective lines are announced through live regions, everything is reachable by keyboard with number keys for choices and Enter to continue, narration is available for the prompts, multiple choice questions are answered with a single selection plus an optional comment, and reduced motion is respected.


## Background notes and further reading

Historical and contextual notes no longer interrupt the activities as automatic cards. Each activity now has a collapsible panel under the dialogue, labeled Background and further reading, that starts with curated outside links, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles and free full texts of the primary sources, and collects the activity's background notes as the student reaches them, to open and close at their choosing.

## Suggested further reading

The activities themselves carry no outside links, so nothing in them can break or lead a student off task. These are for you, and for any student who wants to go further.

- [Doctrine of Double Effect](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/double-effect/)
- [Doing vs. Allowing Harm](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doing-allowing/)
