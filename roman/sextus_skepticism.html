The Physician's Question

What this activity covers

A physician's rooms somewhere in the Roman east, around the year 200. Sextus Empiricus practiced medicine and wrote the fullest surviving account of ancient skepticism, which was not a claim that nothing can be known but a practice, the setting of arguments against arguments until judgment lifts. He agreed to see you between patients on the condition that you bring him something you are certain of. Walk with the arrow keys, press E at each marked stop, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work.

Discussion questions

1. The oldest objection to skepticism is that it refutes itself, since claiming that nothing can be known is itself a knowledge claim. Explain how Sextus tries to escape this, and judge whether the escape works.

2. Modern life requires acting on contested claims constantly, from medical decisions to voting. Describe how someone could use skeptical method responsibly in one such decision, and where the method would have to stop.

3. Sextus promises tranquility as the result of suspending judgment, while the Stoics promise it as the result of correct judgment. Both cannot be describing the same mind. What would count as evidence for one over the other?
