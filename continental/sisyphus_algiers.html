The Myth of Sisyphus

What this activity covers

The first stop in the twentieth century, and a different kind of question. It is 1942, and Albert Camus is writing an essay above the harbour at Algiers about whether life is worth living. He is not asking anyone to answer that about themselves. He is asking what follows when a mind that wants the world to make sense meets a world that returns no answer, a gap he calls the absurd. You walk the hill with him while he defines the word, closes off two ways out, and arrives at a figure pushing a rock. The activity discusses suicide as the philosophical question Camus opens with, and does so briefly and without detail. If the subject is difficult for you, skip the second stop or the activity, and speak to someone you trust. Walk with the arrow keys, press E at each marked spot, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Discussion questions

1. Camus says the absurd needs both a questioner and a silent world, and vanishes if either goes. Test that claim by describing a case where one side is missing, and say whether anything absurd remains.

2. He calls it philosophical suicide to keep the question and assume an answer must be there. Construct the strongest reply on behalf of someone who makes that move, and then say whether it answers him.

3. The essay ends by asking us to imagine Sisyphus happy. Is that a conclusion the argument earns, an image that does work an argument could not, or a change of subject? Defend your answer.
