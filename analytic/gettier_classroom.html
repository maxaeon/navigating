Knowledge, and Three Awkward Cases

What this activity covers

An empty seminar room, a definition that stood for two thousand years, and three examples that break it. Knowledge has long been analysed as justified true belief: you believe it, it is true, and you have good reason. In 1963 a three page paper showed that all three conditions can be met while the person plainly does not know, and philosophers have been patching the definition ever since. You will meet a stopped clock, a white rock that looks like a sheep, and a valley full of barn fronts left behind by a film crew, and then be asked to repair the definition yourself and break your own repair. Walk with the arrow keys, press E at each marked spot, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Discussion questions

1. One popular repair says that nothing false may be doing the work in your reasoning. Test it against all three cases in this activity and report where it succeeds and where it struggles.

2. In the barn case, two people in identical mental states differ in whether they know, because of the surrounding countryside. Is that a reason to reject the case, or a discovery about knowledge?

3. Some philosophers conclude that knowledge cannot be analysed into parts and should be treated as basic. What would be gained and lost by accepting that?
