# Early Modern European Philosophy

The region map for the modern period, drawn on the same traced geography as the world map's west view, with a back button to the world map. Its header states the organizing question plainly, where knowledge comes from, and names the two roads through it, the rationalist answer that reason working from ideas the mind brings with it can establish truths about reality, and the empiricist answer that every material of knowledge arrives through the senses. The stops run in order and jump decades, and each will state its real time and place.

Descartes and the Stove-Heated Room is live. It opens in the winter of 1619 with Descartes quartered in a warm room, pulling his beliefs down on purpose. Five marked spots carry the argument.

At the window the senses are set aside, on the grounds that anything which has deceived once cannot be fully trusted. At the basin the view fills with a close drawing of a stick standing in water, apparently broken at the surface, with the true line of the stick shown dashed beneath the apparent one. The student answers whether sight is simply lying, and Descartes presses the better answer, that sight reported exactly the light that reached the eye and the error entered in the judgment, which is worse for him rather than better. The dream problem follows. At the stove the deceiver hypothesis takes the world, the body, and arithmetic, and the cogito holds, with the student free to answer either that thinking needs a thinker or that thinking alone does not establish a self, an objection Descartes concedes is a good one.

At the wax the view fills again. The wax is shown fresh from the hive with its scent, colour, shape, hardness, and sound listed beside it, and then it melts on the dish while every one of those labels changes to its opposite. The student is asked what they are tracking when they call it the same wax, and Descartes draws his conclusion, that what he grasps is grasped by the mind rather than by any sense. At the desk he names the argument the student is walking into, and the final question asks which road they find more convincing and what their own choice struggles to explain.


Two Objections is live as an optional stop, and it is set in Elisabeth's house rather than Descartes'. The player is at the exiled Bohemian court at The Hague in May 1643, in Elisabeth's own study, and she is the host. She states Descartes' position at its strongest, works out her question with the player's help until it reduces to one clean demand, and sends it. His first reply arrives, the analogy with weight, and she makes the player feel its force before saying what is wrong with it. His second reply arrives, the appeal to a third primitive notion and to knowledge gained by living rather than by intellect, and she refuses it on the grounds that it excuses in his own system exactly what he threw out everywhere else. The activity then continues to a second scene. Anton Wilhelm Amo, born about 1703 at Axim on the coast of what is now Ghana, taken to Europe as a child, and educated at Halle and Wittenberg, defended a disputation in 1734 titled On the Impassivity of the Human Mind. He argued that the mind does not sense at all, that sensation belongs to the living organic body, and that the mind knows the body's states without ever undergoing them. Against Elisabeth, who accepts Descartes' claim that the mind is acted on and asks how, Amo denies the premise and sharpens the division until nothing has to cross it. The activity has him say plainly that this answers only half of her question, since voluntary motion still needs an account.

A point of accuracy that the activity states out loud and that matters for teaching: Elisabeth and Amo never met and could not have. She died in 1680, more than twenty years before he was born, and they were not correspondents. The two scenes are ninety one years apart, and the expedition simply walks the road between them, as it does elsewhere in the Monastery region. What they share is not contact but position, each reading a system built without them and finding the same weak joint. Amo also wrote an earlier work on the legal position of Africans in Europe, now lost, and returned to the Gold Coast around 1747.

The activity is marked optional on the region map, so it never blocks the road, and it does not count toward completing the region.


Hume in Edinburgh is live, and it is the empiricist road. The player walks the city with Hume in 1748 while he lays out three steps. At the printer's window he gives the inventory, impressions and their faint copies, and the rule that every idea traces to an impression, then raises the missing shade of blue against himself and quotes his own dismissal of it, which his critics never let him forget. At the billiard table the view fills with the collision, labelled with the three things actually observed and a line noting that no fourth thing was. On the bridge the problem of induction is built with the student, and whichever premise they offer, it collapses into the same circle. At the inn Hume gives his answer, custom rather than argument, and the strange result that the connection he could not find in the balls turns out to be something in the observer. The walk closes with the man in France who doubted his hands and reached certainty, set against the man who trusted his hands and reached a habit, and with Hume's own backgammon remedy.


Berkeley and the Tree in the Quad is live, and it sits between the two roads. In a Dublin college garden in 1710, Berkeley takes the argument Locke used for secondary qualities and refuses to stop applying it halfway. At the basin a close view shows one hand from cold water and one from hot in the same water, and the student answers whether it is warm or cool. At the sundial he asks what is left of the object once both lists of qualities are in the mind, and issues the master argument, to conceive a wholly unperceived tree, with the standard reply given honestly in the background note. At the elm the tree stays in the empty quad because it is continuously perceived, shown as a steady light over the tree, and Berkeley argues this is where his argument was always going rather than a patch on it. At the gate he insists nothing observable has been removed and that he is defending common sense against the philosophers.

Elisabeth's activity now ends with a visit from Anton Wilhelm Amo. This meeting never happened. Amo was born around 1703, more than twenty years after Elisabeth died in 1680, and there was no contact or correspondence between them. The activity states this plainly in a background note before the scene begins, and the drop-down description on the title says it as well. The scene exists because the two objections belong side by side. Elisabeth asks how an unextended mind could move a body. Amo, in his 1734 Wittenberg dissertation On the Impassivity of the Human Mind, argues that the mind does not sense at all, since sensation belongs to the living organic body, and that calling the mind passive gives it a property only bodies can have. So Elisabeth presses the outbound direction and Amo denies the inbound one, and the activity has Amo concede that his cut leaves her question standing and arguably heavier. Amo was born on the Gold Coast at Axim, took his degree at Halle, taught at Halle and Jena, published a treatise on method in 1738, and returned to West Africa around 1747. An earlier disputation of his on the legal standing of Africans in Europe survives only as a notice of its title.

Berkeley and the Tree in the Quad is live, and it sits between the two roads. Trinity College, Dublin, 1710. Berkeley opens by warning that everyone misunderstands him and that he believes in the tree as firmly as anyone. At the basin the student puts one hand in cold water and one in hot, then both in the same basin, and the view fills with the three vessels and the two opposite reports. That establishes Locke's secondary qualities. At the sundial Berkeley turns the same argument on the primary ones, with a close view of one coin round from above and elliptical from the side, and if the student objects that shape can be measured he answers that measuring is another perception rather than a step outside perception. Under the tree he states the conclusion, that for such a thing to be is to be perceived, and invites the objection. At the chapel door he gives both of his answers about the empty courtyard, the counterfactual one and the one he actually leans on, that God perceives it continually, and the activity notes plainly that this is where most readers stop following him. The closing question asks the student where they would get off the road and what it costs to stay on it.

Kant in Königsberg is live, and it closes the region. The player walks his afternoon route while he lays out the argument in four stages. At the milestone he says plainly what Hume did to him and refuses to pretend it was small, and the student can push him either toward giving up on causes or toward looking outside experience. At the observatory a close view sets the old picture beside the new one, the observer held still with the sky made to fit, and the observer moving with the sky made simple, which is the Copernican turn in his own comparison. At the spectacle maker's window a second close view runs the lens picture, with the same objects plain on one side and carrying the tint on the other, and the student who objects that this leaves us knowing only our lenses is told the objection is the right one. At the cathedral corner he names the price himself, that things in themselves are closed to us, and offers both the trade he thinks it buys and the objection his critics will make within a decade. The closing line is his own, that thoughts without content are empty and intuitions without concepts are blind, and the final question asks the student for their own position on where knowledge comes from.

Finishing Kant opens The Atlantic World on the world map, along with the two twentieth century regions.

Remaining stops, in preparation. Hume in Edinburgh, impressions and ideas, the missing shade of blue, and custom carrying every expectation about tomorrow. Kant in Königsberg, the attempted settlement between the two roads. Wollstonecraft in London, the era's confidence in reason applied to the era's own arrangements. Completion keys will be `phil_map_descartes`, `phil_map_hume`, `phil_map_kant`, and `phil_map_wollstonecraft`, and completing all four will complete the region, which together with the Monastery opens The Atlantic World.
