Kant in Königsberg

What this activity covers

The last stop on both roads, and the one that tries to end the quarrel. It is 1781, and Immanuel Kant has published almost nothing for eleven years while working on a book that will run to eight hundred pages. He agrees with Hume that experience cannot deliver necessity, and refuses to conclude that we should give up on causes. His answer is to turn the question round and ask what the world must be like in order to be experienced by minds built as ours are. You walk his afternoon route with him while he lays it out, and he names the price at the end rather than hiding it. Best played after Descartes, Hume, and Berkeley. Walk with the arrow keys, press E at each marked spot, and answer each question when it appears. At the end you can review your responses, answer discussion questions, take a short quiz, and download a report of your work. You can skip any question you would rather not answer. In course mode, which your instructor may set for you, the last question asks for a written answer.

Your responses at each stage

Discussion questions

1. Kant claims some things can be known in advance of experience because they are conditions of any experience. Pick one such claim, that every event has a cause or that experience is in time, and try to describe what it would be like to encounter a counterexample. What does the attempt show?

2. His critics said within a decade that the thing in itself should be dropped. Argue the case for dropping it, then the case for keeping it, and say which you find stronger.

3. Descartes, Hume, Berkeley, and Kant all agree that we do not have unmediated access to the world. Set out what each thinks stands between us and it, and say whether they are really answering the same question.
